Three years ago, I started contemplating starting a side business as a life coach. To prepare for that role, I decided it would be a good idea to practice being a life coach to myself.

<!--more-->

Since I took on this challenge, I’ve accumulated a size-able collection of self-coach notes with raw lessons and ideas I extracted from my daily experiences. Most of these entries are prescriptive while others are observational or reflections about mortality and impermanence. There are 100 distinct ideas in the list. The ideas are inspired by:

* My experiences overcoming difficult situations
* Learning new hobbies (violin)
* Overcoming challenges (successfully or unsuccessfully)
* Self reflection during work performance review cycle
* Listening to other people’s stories and counseling them
* Things I heard from blog posts, YouTube, or social media that added clarity and inspired me
* Things I learned in a specific context (e.g, yoga or sports) that have analogues in other contexts (e.g., work) and have larger lessons for life in general

Recently I stumbled upon Marcus Aurelius’s book Meditations.  I realized that my “life coach” notes list is the same.

Marcus Aurelius was a Stoic. I never formally studied Stoicism before but the more I look into it, the more I realize I’ve been a Stoic at heart all along. Because I was raised with Buddhist and Zen beliefs, the golden rule "Don't worry about things you can't control; focus on things you can control" has always been a fundamental principle shaping how I live my life.

When Marcus Aurelius wrote his journal entries, he never intended for them to be published. They were private, unpolished notes written for his eyes only. My "life coach" list is the exact same thing: unpolished reminders I wrote strictly for myself. Because they are personal notes (meditations), a lot of them have no context recorded.

Some meditations are way longer than others. The verbosity can be attributed to one of the two reasons (1) the lesson is not so obvious or it’s hard to see how it can be generally applicable without some explanation or examples, (2) the idea is controversial or paradigm-shifting and took some internal dialogue to convince myself of their validity.

I am publishing my notes exactly as written, just like Marcus Aurelius's notes. Here are my first 100 meditations. The list is ordered by recency (most recent entry is at the top and has the smaller number).

## Meditations

1. Don’t take a risk you can’t handle. Before taking a risk, ask: what’s the worst that can happen if I fail and am I ok with the consequences in the worse case scenario? Would I have to sell my kidney to make ends meet? In reality, it’s never this bad for most people. But they may lose self confidence or sense of self worth if they failed and have to move back in with their parents. For some people, that is almost as bad as losing a kidney. Every person has a different threshold for what’s too much risk.
2. One-way door vs two way door decisions. Always prefer two-way-door but have a rollback strategy before implementing that decision.
3. There’s no point trying to argue with someone who is religious about something and is preachy about it. Example: argument with a friend about the best way to exercise. They will not listen to your point of view and be or acknowledge that the best way to exercise is different for every person. They are religious about their way because they did a lot of research into it and their way worked out really well for them. They want to convert you because they want to “help you” or “enlighten you”.  It doesn’t mean they can’t still be your friend. You just need to have empathy that they can’t help their moralizing behavior when you talk to them about something they are super passionate about. The best thing to do in this situation is to change the topic.
4. Being productive is the wrong metric for success. Aim for being impactful. The best case is being impactful with the least amount of work. Figure out how to maximize impact to work ratio should be a top priority.
5. “follow your passion” is not sustainable because it is motivated by an emotion which fades over time. Figure out a way to make the thing you want to do automatic and easy so it becomes a habit, routine, and a part of your identity.  It’s easy to do what feels good and is easy.
6. Be a master of your biology, not a slave to it. In a stressful situations, our body gets into a fight-or-flight response. The biological benefit of fight-or-flight response is immediate survival, but it comes at a high cost to your long term health if you are constantly in fight-or-flight state. The cortisol and adrenaline baseline levels get permanently elevated, which causes cumulative, systematic damage to all your major organs. Sustained high levels of cortisol is toxic to your brain and cortisols also damages your metabolism by continually signaling your body to conserve and store energy, leading to increased appetite, cravings for high-fat and high-sugar foods. In survival situation, you need all that fat and sugar to run away from predators. In a corporate environment and modern life, you are not actually running away from predators. Don’t let anything at work or in life raise your cortisol level.
7. (DearModern/Cliff Tan) Stop complaining, fix it. Your environment (home, work/life situation) is a thing you can control. It’s one thing to be dropped into an unpleasant situation, it’s another thing to allow yourself to stay in that situation and cope by complaining. Unexpected work comes up that requires attention. Don’t complain and feel bad that you have more work than you originally thought. Either just do the work, or say no, or find someone else to do it.
8. A lot of work doesn’t need to be done because they are anticipatory or prep work based on incomplete understanding of the problem and wrong assumptions. If you are not sure the work will add value, just wait until the motivation and scope becomes more clear and certain. Don’t “be proactive” and do anticipatory work that becomes negative work (work that you have to undo later and causes more harm than good that you have to clean up later). Don’t do work that will hurt other people or your future self. Just wait and let the work definition become clearer. Often, the work becomes no longer required because people who asked to do the work realized that they don’t actually need this work to be done and it doesn’t move the needle on the KPI. Not waiting for clearer scope definitions and jumping in too early often causes more harm than good because it creates lock-in on the wrong solution (sunk cost fallacy) that adds friction for actually doing the right thing. And you have to maintain fragile and poorly designed architecture and spend all your time fixing bugs.
9. Spend time with yourself - you make time for your work, loved ones, causes you care about. Why not also make time for yourself? Have a conversation with yourself to understand your worries, priorities, and values. Allow yourself to go into rabbit holes to scratch your curiosity itch. A lot of people are willing to spend a lot of time cooking nutritious meals for their kids/loved ones but they are not willing to cook a healthy meal for themselves and opt for takeouts. Why is it not worth your time to make yourself happy and healthy? Think: you are your own child. You wouldn’t neglect and abuse your child, why would you neglect and abuse yourself?
10. The North Star for prioritization - make people’s life easier. Prioritize doing things and adopt behavior that moves the needle the most in making people’s life easier. Make your coworkers’ life easier by working on automation and tools and prioritize tasks to unblock them. Make customer’s life easier by shipping things they need and fixing bugs that are blocking them from doing their job (if you work on enterprise software). Make your boss’s life easier by delivering on schedule, communicate clearly, know when to ask for permission and when to ask for forgiveness so they don’t have to chase down answers and have difficult conversations with stakeholders and their bosses. Every time you have to make a decision, it drains mental energy. Make other people’s life easier by removing amount of small and inconsequential decisions they have to make (taking initiative, proposing solution and alternatives and ask for feedback. This would simplify the decision they have to make to a yes/no answer). Make your own life easier. Want to live healthier? Remove all the frictions you have to exercise good habit. When I moved, I quit a gym that was 15 minute walk from me and joined a more expensive gym that’s only a 5 minute walk from me. I’m saving 20 minutes everyday going to and back from the gym. Extra 20 minute walk is friction. It’s the difference between going to the gym or not going during my hour lunch break.  Remove the choices you have to make about whether certain processed food is good for you based on the quantity of the bad ingredients. This drains mental energy. Just adopt a zero tolerance policy - I only eat whole foods that are not processed or minimally processed foods with three or fewer ingredients (I also have a restriction that I can only eat food I cook myself). It’s not binary thinking. I understand the nuance and there are gray areas. I’m just choosing to live with these self-imposed restrictions so I don’t have to make a lot of tiny decisions everyday to conserve valuable mental energy.
11. When you want to voice your frustration/complain to someone, ask yourself - what is the point of saying this to them? Is it to make them feel bad about themselves? Is it to make them feel bad for you? Is it to make them feel angry, sad, or any negative emotions even if they have nothing to do with it? If the answer to any of these questions is yes, don’t do it. Thats a manipulative. You risk ruin someone’s day for no good reason at all. Find another way to vocalize your concern so it’s actionable and say it to the people that can take those actions so the underlying situation that causes your frustration can be changed.
12. how to navigate angry situations:  in any debate or discussion, there are two kinds of people - the soldier and the scout. Ask yourself this - what would i need to see to be proven wrong and how would I feel when that happens? Aristotle lays out three methods of persuasion - logos (based on logic), ethos (based on their character and authority), and pathos (based on emotion).
13. How society brainwashes us: Are you a protagonist in your life? Or NPC or supporting character? I spend a lot of time thinking, planning, and understanding these days. The danger of living in a hyper-capitalist society:  Instead of asking ourself what do we want out of life, we get this pre-packaged, curated list of answers from society. Asian Dad Energy [puts it](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Wv4Y1Rcaj5c) best: “My day is jam packed with things to do but few of those things actually achieved anything meaningful to me. Maybe the things that I spent somuch time working on had a purpose, but it was not my purpose. Because nearly all of my time was spent working, I didn’t have the free time and energy to go out there and seek my own purpose and meaning.” So in this way, our hyper capitalist society shapes who we think we are. Productivity becomes identity. Material consumption becomes fulfillment. Relationships becomes a status symbol. And career success gets tied to intrinsic self-worth. Hyper-capitalism doesn’t just make us buy a lot of crap. It shapes our values, our ambitions, and our aspirations. It tells us what we should want for life before we even had a chance to find out what it is we want for ourselves. Ultimately, it defines what a good life is supposed to look like on our behalf and without our permission.

How to brainwash yourself - Cherry picking to support a narrative is unscientific; however, if your goal is not research and science but rather to change you belief system (faith), then pick a narrative and cherry pick is a technique to rewire your brain. This is how brainwashing works. Radical habit change is like brain-washing yourself! Learn from the brain-washing masters and cult leaders to study their techniques and do it on yourself (be careful yo only do it on yourself and not other people because it’s unethical). Sometimes strong beliefs and faith are required to do the hard things. We are naturally wired to avoid doing hard things. If you want to change that, you have to build your religion for which you are your sole follower. Examples: (1) Narrative: “I’m an athlete”. (2) Belief system is updated, write your new doctrine and indoctrinate yourself into your new routine. There’s a ceremony of going to the gym everyday and devote yourself to a strict diet. Break every problem down and work on individual building blocks of the sound (finger placement and bow use) in isolation before putting it together. You can also assume temporary personas. Have a bug and production support issue? Put on your investigator hat. (3) Narrative: “I’m a detective”. Do what Sherlock Holmes does. Come up with hypotheses, follow leads, have a system of process of elimination. The more you do this, the easier it is to “flip the switch”. Practice brainwashing yourself.
